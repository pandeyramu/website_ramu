CEE Eligibility with Low GPA: Can You Apply with D Grades?

A relative of mine finished +2 with a D in Chemistry and assumed his entrance journey was over before it started. I spent an evening reading notices and calling admission desks with him. This post is what we found, written down so someone else does not have to start from zero.

By Ramu Pandey · Published: August 13, 2026 · 8 min read

Every few weeks someone asks this question in a student group: "I have a low GPA, can I still sit for CEE?" The answers underneath are usually confident and frequently wrong. Some people say any grade works as long as you pass the entrance. Others insist a single D disqualifies you everywhere. When my cousin finished his +2 with a D in Chemistry and a decent overall result, I decided to stop guessing and actually check.

What follows is not official advice. Rules change year to year and differ between institutions, so treat this as a map of what to verify rather than a verdict. But the process of checking taught me a lot about how eligibility actually works, and most of it was more encouraging than the comment sections suggest.

What eligibility usually depends on

Entrance examinations in Nepal generally attach two kinds of academic requirements. The first is completion of a qualifying degree: for most science and health entrances that means 10+2 with Physics, Chemistry, and Biology or Mathematics depending on the programme. The second is a minimum level of performance in that degree, expressed either as an aggregate GPA, minimum grades in specific subjects, or sometimes both.

The details are set by whoever runs the examination and by the institutions admitting through it. That is why two students with identical marksheets can face different answers depending on where they apply. One university's notice may ask for a certain aggregate with no subject-wise floor. Another may require a minimum grade specifically in the science subjects relevant to the programme. Neither approach is universal, and neither stays frozen across years.

The one document that matters: the official admission notice published for your exam cycle, on the website of the body conducting the exam. Not a YouTube video, not a Facebook comment, not even this post. Notices are short, and the eligibility clause is almost always in the first few sections.

The D grade question specifically

Under the NEB letter grading system, a D is technically a passing grade. Whether it satisfies an entrance requirement depends entirely on the rule attached to the programme you want. From what I read while helping my cousin, three patterns come up repeatedly.

  • Aggregate-only rules: some programmes look at the overall GPA and do not impose a floor on individual subjects. A strong aggregate can carry a weak subject.
  • Subject-wise floors: many health-science programmes require a minimum grade in Physics, Chemistry, and Biology individually, precisely because those subjects carry the entrance syllabus. A D in one of them can block these programmes even when the aggregate looks fine.
  • Weighted or relaxed categories: a few pathways treat technical SLC/SEE streams, diploma holders, or other backgrounds differently, sometimes with additional requirements instead of a straight GPA cut.

My cousin's D was in Chemistry. For engineering-oriented programmes at several institutions, his aggregate cleared the bar and the D did not matter much. For the health-science route he originally wanted, the subject-wise requirement was the obstacle. Same marksheet, two different futures. That contrast taught me that "am I eligible?" is never one question. It is one question per programme.

Options if your grades fall short

Finding out that a particular door is closed for this cycle is disappointing, but it is rarely the end of the road. These were the realistic paths we identified.

Grade improvement examination. NEB allows students to reappear to improve grades in specific subjects. If a single subject is what blocks your preferred programme, improving that one grade is often faster and cheaper than people assume. The trade-off is time: you need the improved result before the admission deadline of your target cycle, so the timing has to be planned backwards from the notice dates.

Different programmes under the same exam family. Entrance systems in Nepal are not monolithic. Programmes with heavy Biology requirements tend to be stricter about Biology grades. Engineering and some applied-science programmes weight Mathematics and Physics more. A lower score in one subject may matter enormously for one faculty and barely at all for another.

Diploma-to-degree routes. Several universities admit diploma holders into bachelor programmes through their own criteria. If your school-side record is weak but your interest is genuine, a diploma with a strong result followed by a degree pathway sometimes beats fighting the GPA rule directly.

A gap cycle used deliberately. One clean cycle spent fixing exactly one weakness, whether that is a grade or entrance readiness, is common among students I studied with. Nobody asks in a job interview how many months sat between your +2 and your bachelor degree.

How this connects to entrance preparation itself

There is one more thing worth saying. Eligibility gets you a seat in the exam hall. It does nothing about what happens in the three hours after the bell. Students who scraped through +2 with weak fundamentals sometimes walk into entrance preparation carrying gaps from topics their class skipped or rushed. That is fixable, but only if you diagnose it early instead of discovering it in a mock test.

When my cousin and I finally sat down to study, we began with chapter wise practice instead of full papers, precisely because his fundamentals were uneven. Working through questions topic by topic exposed the gaps quickly, and the explanations on this site filled a lot of them without extra textbooks. If your grades are shaky, I would honestly start the same way rather than jumping into full-length tests and taking damage to your confidence every weekend. A sensible sequence is described in my guide on preparing for CEE effectively, and the chapter weightings in the marks distribution breakdown help you spend effort where questions actually cluster.

Low grades say something about how school went. They do not fix your ceiling. The entrance exam resets the scoreboard for everyone in the hall, eligible or not, and preparation counts for far more than history once the question paper is in front of you.

One warning: agents and consultancies occasionally charge money for "eligibility guarantees". Eligibility is determined by published rules, not by anyone's promise. Before paying anyone, read the notice yourself or call the institution's admission desk directly. It costs nothing and takes ten minutes.

The checklist we ended up using

  1. Write down the exact programmes you care about, not just "CEE".
  2. Find the latest official notice for each and note its aggregate and subject-wise clauses.
  3. Mark which requirement, if any, your marksheet fails today.
  4. If it is one subject, price out the grade improvement route against the notice calendar.
  5. If eligibility is clear, stop worrying about it and put all energy into preparation.

That last step matters more than it sounds. Half the anxiety around eligibility comes from re-checking rumours after the answer is already known. Read the notice once, make your decision, close the tab, and go solve practice questions. Your rank will argue for you better than your GPA ever could.
