MAT Micro Syllabus for CEE: Every Question Type Explained

Most guides say "practise reasoning" and stop there. I wanted the actual list. This is the full micro syllabus of the MAT section as I mapped it during preparation, organised into its four reasoning areas with a note on how each question type behaves.

By Ramu Pandey · Published: August 17, 2026 · 9 min read

When I started preparing the Mental Ability Test portion of CEE, the advice I found was always the same shape: "MAT is about practice." True, but useless. Practice on what? Which types exist? Which ones eat time? Nobody published the list, so eventually I built it myself, topic by topic, mostly by working through question banks until the categories stopped surprising me.

The result is below. Everything falls into four areas: logical sequencing, numerical reasoning, spatial or abstract reasoning, and verbal reasoning. Under them sit dozens of specific question types. Learning to recognise the type instantly is most of the battle, because each type has exactly one solving routine.

Area 1: Logical Sequencing

This area is pure pattern hunting. No formulas anywhere, which makes it the friendliest starting point.

  • Number Series: a sequence with one missing term; you test differences, ratios, squares, and alternating patterns until one fits.
  • Alphabet Series: same game on letters; positions in the alphabet (A=1 through Z=26) must become reflex.
  • Analogy: "A is to B as C is to ?" You copy the relationship, not the objects.
  • Classification: find the odd one out; three items share a property the fourth lacks.
  • Common Properties: pick the option that shares the stated property with the given set.
  • Logical Sequence of Words: arrange words in a natural order, such as stages of a process or parts of a whole.
  • Logical Venn-Diagram: decide how groups overlap and match the correct circle diagram.
  • Matrix and Missing Characters: a grid of letters or numbers governed by a hidden row-column rule.
  • Continuous Patterns and Positional Series: tracking a repeating pattern across positions, common in slightly harder sets.

Nearly everything here should fall under a minute once trained. If a series resists two passes of testing, mark and move; the next question will not care that this one beat you.

Area 2: Numerical Reasoning

These are Class 8-to-10 arithmetic topics wearing entrance-exam costumes. The maths is never advanced; the twist density is what varies.

  • Percentage: conversions between fractions and percentages appear everywhere, often hiding inside other questions.
  • Ratio and Proportion: dividing amounts in given ratios; the backbone of many word problems.
  • Averages: changes to averages when members join, leave, or get corrected.
  • Profit and Loss: cost price, selling price, markup and discount chains.
  • Simple Interest and Compound Interest: formula recall plus two-year shortcut patterns.
  • Time and Work: rates adding up; pipes filling and emptying tanks together.
  • Time, Speed and Distance: the classic trio, including relative speed cases.
  • Problem on Ages: age relationships now versus some years earlier or later.
  • Partnership: profit split by capital invested and time period.
  • Permutation and Combination: counting selections and arrangements; small numbers, careful reading.

Fraction-percentage pairs are leverage: knowing that 1/8 = 12.5% or 1/6 ≈ 16.67% turns several slow calculations across this entire area into instant answers. Twenty minutes of memorising buys marks in five different question types.

Area 3: Spatial Relation / Abstract Reasoning

The visual area, and the one students fear most before trying it. On paper it looks alien; after a week of drilling it becomes pattern matching like everything else. It contains the largest pile of distinct types.

  • Paper Folding: a sheet folded and punched; predict the unfolded hole pattern.
  • Cube and Dice: painted cubes cut into smaller ones, or dice face logic.
  • Water Image and Mirror Image: reflections of figures and words along horizontal or vertical axes.
  • Embedded Figure: find a small figure hiding inside the larger one.
  • Figure Formation: which complete figure can be assembled from the given pieces.
  • Construction of Figure: counting triangles, squares, or other shapes inside a composite diagram.
  • Grouping Figure: sort scattered figures into meaningful groups.
  • Dot Situation: place a dot in the region satisfying several circle conditions at once.
  • Matrix: figures arranged in a grid following a visual rule, one cell blank.
  • Series (figures): pictures advancing by steps; choose the next frame.
  • Analogy (figures): the relationship copied between shapes instead of words.
  • Classification Test: the odd figure among a set.
  • Analytic Reasoning Test: multi-clue puzzles about positions and arrangements solved with rough sketches.

My mistake was treating these as talent. They are not. Fold a real piece of paper once for Paper Folding and the printed version stops being abstract forever. Physical props work absurdly well in this area.

Area 4: Verbal Reasoning Test

Language-flavoured logic plus tiny arithmetic puzzles.

  • Verbal Analogy and Verbal Classification: the Area 1 ideas expressed in words.
  • Synonym and Antonym: vocabulary checks; built slowly by reading, not crammed overnight.
  • Coding and Decoding: messages encoded by letter shifts or position swaps; decode the rule, apply it.
  • Blood Relations: family trees drawn from sentences; sketching beats staring.
  • Distance and Direction: walks described turn by turn; final displacement from start.
  • Ranking Order: people standing in lines, someone's position counted from both ends.
  • Arithmetical Operation: small equations where operators or digits substitute for symbols.
  • Statement and Reasons: judge conclusions against given statements; resist importing outside knowledge.
  • Verbal Puzzle: mixed short puzzles that borrow from all of the above.

How to actually train it

One type per sitting beats random mixing early on. When I drilled Cube and Dice alone for three evenings, accuracy climbed fast because every repetition reused the same mental move. Mixing types too soon forces constant gear-switching and hides your weak spots.

Once every type has had its own focused pass, mix them under time pressure, because the exam never labels the type for you. Recognition speed becomes the skill. That final stage is also where negative marking discipline matters, since rushed guesses in MAT feel harmless and quietly bleed marks; my rules for that live in the negative marking strategy post, and the broader approach to the section is in the MAT scoring guide.

Every type named above has dedicated chapter wise practice on this site, grouped under the same four headings, so the list doubles as a navigation aid: drill one bullet at a time until it bores you, then move down the list. Boredom is roughly the signal that a type has transferred from "thinking" to "recognition", which is exactly where you want it before exam day.
