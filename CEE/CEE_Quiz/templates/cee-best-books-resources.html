CEE Preparation Books and Resources: What I Used and What I Skipped

My first month of preparation, I bought too many books. Some carried me to the finish line; others were expensive paperweights. Here is the honest inventory, so your shelf starts leaner than mine did.

By Ramu Pandey · Published: August 18, 2026 · 8 min read

Walk into any bookshop around a college area in exam season and you will find a wall of entrance-preparation volumes, each thicker and more optimistic than the last. I bought six of them in my first two weeks. By the end of preparation, I was genuinely using three. This post is the inventory of everything I tried, sorted by what actually repaid its cost.

The principle before the list

Entrance exams test recall speed under time pressure. Textbooks teach depth. Both matter, but they answer different questions, and confusing them is how students end up reading theory for four months and walking into the hall having attempted almost nothing timed. My rule after trial and error became simple: one reference per subject at most, then everything else is question practice.

Physics

For concepts I kept a standard calculus-free physics text from my +2 years and used it only when a derivation genuinely confused me. The workhorse was a well-known objective physics volume organised by topic with short theory capsules followed by large MCQ sets. That format matches the exam's shape almost exactly.

The mistake to avoid: buying multiple objective volumes for the same subject. Two overlapping MCQ banks do not double your exposure; they duplicate it while halving your revision time.

Chemistry

Chemistry split cleanly into three behaviours for me. Physical problems needed worked numericals, which any standard physical chemistry chapter set provided. Inorganic was pure memorisation, and no book fixed that better than my own condensed notes; I transferred facts onto fewer and fewer pages until one sheet covered the whole section. Organic benefited most from reaction maps rather than linear reading, a method I described separately in the organic reactions post and the reaction map guide.

Inorganic truth: no amount of re-reading beats self-testing on inorganic facts. Reading a colour chart ten times loses to covering it and recalling it three times. Books give you the facts; retention comes from your own quizzes.

Biology

A single NCERT-level biology text plus one objective biology volume handled nearly everything. Diagrams mattered more than any prose, which turned out to be the subject's defining feature; my whole approach there eventually became redrawing figures from memory, written up in the diagram revision post.

MAT

Any decent verbal-and-non-verbal reasoning compilation covers the types. The specific brand matters far less than structured drilling by type, which I explained in the MAT micro syllabus breakdown. If money is tight, this is the easiest subject to prepare almost free, because every question type follows learnable routines available in any library copy or older edition.

What I skipped without regret

  • Thick "complete guide" omnibus editions: too broad, too shallow, and they duplicate what subject-wise volumes do better.
  • Multiple current-affairs style supplements: irrelevant for a science entrance.
  • Premium coaching packages bought late: material arrives faster than you can absorb it near the deadline. Late-stage preparation needs revision of your own notes, not new stacks.
  • Collector instinct: the belief that the missing seventh book holds something vital. It does not. Unfinished books teach nothing regardless of quality.

The stack I would buy today

  1. One objective volume per science subject (Physics, Chemistry, Biology), recent edition, any established publisher.
  2. One reasoning compilation for MAT.
  3. A past-paper collection, used as diagnostic material rather than trophy practice, as described in the past papers post.
  4. Nothing else until one of those four is finished.

Four items. Everything else on the shop wall is optional by definition, because the binding constraint in entrance preparation has never been access to questions. It is completed, reviewed, revised questions, and a lean stack makes those far easier to accumulate. For turning whichever stack you choose into a weekly routine, the plan in the final 30 days post shows the shape of the last stretch.
