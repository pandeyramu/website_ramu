CEE Daily Study Routine: A 10-11 Hour Day That Actually Works

This is the exact day schedule I followed while preparing for CEE: about 10-11 focused study hours a day, six days a week, a full mock test every Saturday, and a strict 7 hours of sleep every night. Here is how I built the routine, hour by hour, and why it held up for months.

By Ramu Pandey · Published: June 29, 2026 · 10 min read

For a long time I thought preparing well for CEE meant studying as many hours as my body could survive. My first attempt at a timetable was colour-coded, hour-by-hour, and covered every topic across Physics, Chemistry, Biology, and MAT. It looked impressive on paper. It lasted eleven days. I had scheduled no room for sleep or rest, and by the second week I was exhausted, falling behind, and guilty about the whole thing.

The routine I eventually settled on and kept for the rest of my preparation was different in three specific ways. It had a fixed number of hours each day instead of an endless list. It treated sleep as a non-negotiable part of study, not an enemy. And it reserved one full day every week for a mock test. That routine is what I describe below, and it is built around real marks distribution rather than preference.

The marks first: why I schedule time this way

Everything in my daily routine follows the actual CEE marks distribution. The exam is 200 questions, and Biology (Zoology + Botany) carries 80 marks, Physics carries 50, Chemistry carries 50, and MAT carries 20. That single fact decides how I split my hours. Giving all four subjects equal time would mean massively over-investing in MAT and under-investing in Biology.

Here is how the marks translate into a daily share of study time.

SubjectMarksPercentageShare of daily study time
Biology (Zoology + Botany)8040%4 hours/day
Physics5025%2.5 hours/day
Chemistry5025%2.5 hours/day
MAT2010%1 hour/day

This is a baseline, not a prison. If a subject is weak, the extra time comes from the subjects carrying fewer marks. But with a realistic 10-11 hours a day, this split keeps me covering every subject daily while respecting that Biology is where most of the marks live.

The two non-negotiables: Sleep 7 hours a night, no exceptions, and take one full mock test every Saturday. These two habits did more for my score than any extra hour of cramming. A tired brain forgets answers it already knew, and a mock test every week tells you exactly where you stand before it matters.

My day routine: the 10-11 hour schedule

On the six study days (Sunday to Friday) I work in blocks, not as one endless stretch. Each block is 45 to 50 minutes of focused work with a 10 minute break. I never study a single subject for more than about 2.5 hours in a row because concentration drops sharply after that. Here is the shape of an average day.

TimeActivityNotes
6:00 - 7:00Wake up, light exercise, breakfastNo screen time yet
7:00 - 9:00Biology - new theory topic (2 hrs)Fresh mind for high-weightage subject
9:00 - 11:30Physics - theory + numericals (2.5 hrs)With a break inside
11:30 - 12:30Lunch and short restStepping away on purpose
12:30 - 2:30Chemistry - theory / inorganic (2 hrs)Active recall, not re-reading
2:30 - 4:00Biology - Botany + MCQ practice (1.5 hrs)Convert theory into marks
4:00 - 5:00MAT - practice sets (1 hr)Short but daily, keep it sharp
5:00 - 6:30Break, walk, dinnerGenuine rest
6:30 - 8:30Physics - numericals / weak topics (2 hrs)Second pass on the day's hard stuff
8:30 - 9:30MCQ practice + error log update (1 hr)End the day testing, not reading
10:30Sleep7 hours, consistently

That adds up to roughly 11 hours of actual study. On days when I feel worn out, I drop the evening Physics block and keep the rest, which brings it to about 10 hours. The daily structure stays the same; only one block flexes. The important thing is that every high-weightage subject is touched every single day.

Saturday: the mock test day

Saturday is a study day too, but a different kind. I take one full length CEE mock test, 200 questions with negative marking and a timer, exactly like the real exam. I sit for it at the same time of day I expect to sit the actual CEE, usually the morning, so my body gets used to performing at that hour.

The rest of Saturday is spent on error analysis. I go through every wrong answer, write down the reason I missed it, and list the specific topics that caused the mistakes. This is the most valuable hour of my entire week. After the analysis I stop early and sleep properly, because Sunday is a fresh six-day cycle.

Mock tests are practice, not punishment: A low mock score on Saturday is information, not failure. Track the score week to week and focus on the trend. My score climbed steadily once I stopped judging each test and started mining it for reasons to adjust the coming week's routine.

Why sleep gets a fixed slot in the schedule

I used to cut sleep to add study hours, and my mock scores barely moved. The mistake is easy to make: an extra hour of study feels productive while an hour of sleep feels wasted. But memory consolidation happens during sleep, and a sleep-deprived brain performs measurably worse on the kind of recall questions CEE asks.

I built the day backwards from sleep. I decided I wanted to be in bed by 10:30 and wake at 5:30, which gives 7 hours. From there I planned the study blocks backwards. That single ordering decision made the rest of the routine hold together. Sleep stopped being whatever was left over and became the anchor of the day.

The revision that is built into the routine

New topics are only half of the routine. Every evening MCQ block and every Saturday analysis is deliberate revision. My rule is simple: a topic I studied today has to be tested today. If I cannot answer MCQs on it by the end of the day, I did not really learn it, and it goes back into tomorrow's weak-topics slot.

I keep a topic tracker, one line per topic, and mark each of the three passes: first study, first revision, second revision. Topics that still fail MCQs after three passes get moved to the Saturday analysis and repeated the following week. This system is boring on purpose. It is designed to make sure nothing quietly slips out of memory.

When the routine breaks

Real life happens. In one week I was sick for three days and the routine simply did not run. I did not panic and I did not try to recover all the lost hours in a single day. I dropped the optional evening block for a few days, protected sleep, and let the Saturday mock and the weak-topics slot catch the material up.

The lesson is that a routine survives because it has flexible parts. The fixed core is sleep, the Saturday mock, and touching every subject daily. Everything else can shrink. If you are consistently failing to reach the full day, do not add more hours. Protect the core, cut a block, and keep showing up. Consistency across months beats intensity across weeks every single time.

What I would tell myself on day one

The routine only works if it is built on the actual marks distribution and a realistic number of hours. CEE is 80 marks of Biology, 25 each to Physics, 25 each to Chemistry, and only 10 to MAT. Study 10-11 focused hours across six days, mock test on Saturday, and sleep 7 hours every night. That is the whole strategy.

Build the day backwards from sleep. Allocate hours by marks, not preference. Test what you study the same day. And treat the Saturday mock as a weekly report card, not a verdict. The exam measures what you remember and how accurately you apply it. A sustainable daily routine is the most reliable way to build both.
