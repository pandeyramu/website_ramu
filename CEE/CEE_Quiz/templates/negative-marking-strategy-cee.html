CEE Negative Marking Strategy: When to Skip a Question

CEE deducts 0.25 marks for every wrong answer. That single rule changes the entire game. Here is the skip-vs-guess framework I developed after losing marks to careless guessing in my first attempt.

By Ramu Pandey · Published: July 26, 2026 · 8 min read

In my first CEE attempt, I treated negative marking as an inconvenience. I knew that each wrong answer cost 0.25 marks, but I did not let that knowledge shape my behaviour during the exam. I guessed when I felt uncertain, I changed answers on instinct, and I filled in blanks in the final minutes just because they were blank. When the results came back, my score was 14 marks lower than my best mock test. The difference was almost entirely wrong guesses that I did not need to make.

Negative marking is not just a penalty. It is a system that rewards disciplined decision-making and punishes impulsive guessing. Once I learned to treat every blank question as a calculated choice rather than a problem to fix, my scores stabilised. This guide breaks down the exact framework I used.

Understanding the math behind 0.25

Every correct answer in CEE earns you 1 mark. Every wrong answer costs you 0.25 marks. A blank answer costs nothing. This means four wrong answers completely cancel out one correct answer. The question you need to ask before guessing is simple: is my probability of being right high enough to justify the risk?

If you are guessing randomly among four options, your chance of being correct is 25%. The expected value of a random guess is 0.25 times 1 minus 0.75 times 0.25, which equals zero. Random guessing on a four-option question with 0.25 negative marking has an expected value of exactly zero. It does not help you and it does not hurt you on average. But exam days are not average days. Stress, fatigue, and time pressure push you toward wrong guesses more often than right ones.

The skip threshold: Only guess when you can eliminate at least two options. If you can narrow a question down to two choices, your probability of being correct rises to 50%. The expected value becomes 0.5 times 1 minus 0.5 times 0.25, which equals 0.375. That is a net positive. If you cannot eliminate two options, skip the question. A blank is always better than a coin flip.

How I calculated my personal risk tolerance

Not every student has the same risk tolerance, and your strategy should reflect your own confidence level. I built a simple table during my preparation that tracked my accuracy rate on practice tests for each subject. Here is what my data looked like after five full length mock CEE papers:

SubjectMy Accuracy RateStrategy
Biology78%Attempt all, including uncertain ones where I can eliminate one option
Physics55%Attempt only confident answers and 50-50 guesses after elimination
Chemistry62%Attempt confident answers, skip pure Inorganic guesses
MAT70%Attempt all except time-consuming puzzles

This data told me something important: I should be aggressive with Biology and MAT where my accuracy was high, but conservative with Physics where nearly half my answers were wrong on practice tests. Blind guessing in Physics was literally costing me marks. This subject-specific approach is more useful than a single blanket rule.

Specific examples from my CEE experience

During the actual CEE, I encountered a Genetics question about dihybrid cross ratios that I had not revised thoroughly. I remembered that the F2 ratio was 9:3:3:1 but could not recall whether the question was asking for the phenotypic or genotypic ratio in a specific scenario. I could eliminate options A and D because they did not match the standard ratio format, leaving B and C. That was a 50-50 situation. I attempted it and got it right.

Later in the Physics section, a question asked about the time period of a simple pendulum on a planet with different gravitational acceleration. I had no formula memory for that specific variation. I could not eliminate any option with confidence. I skipped it. That skip saved me 0.25 marks compared to the student next to me who guessed and got it wrong.

In the Chemistry section, an Organic Chemistry question asked about the major product of an electrophilic addition reaction. I remembered the general mechanism but was unsure about the regioselectivity rule. I could eliminate two options based on the molecular structure, so I attempted one of the remaining two. I was wrong, but the loss was only 0.25 marks instead of the 0.75 marks I would have lost if I had been unable to eliminate any options and guessed randomly.

The three-level decision system

I used a simple three-level system for every uncertain question during CEE. This system removed emotional decision-making from the process and replaced it with a clear rule.

  • Level 1 - Fully confident: I know the answer or I am at least 80% sure. Attempt immediately. Do not come back to these.
  • Level 2 - Eliminated to two options: I can rule out two of the four choices with reasonable logic. Attempt during the second pass. The math supports this decision.
  • Level 3 - Cannot eliminate or barely eliminated one: Skip it. Do not return. A blank is worth zero. A wrong answer is worth negative 0.25. Do the math.

The key discipline is sticking to Level 3 decisions even when the clock is running down. In my first attempt, I abandoned this system in the final 15 minutes and guessed on seven questions. Five were wrong. I lost 1.25 marks in a panic that felt like it would help but actually hurt.

The panic guess trap: In the final minutes of CEE, the urge to fill every blank feels urgent. Resist it. Random guessing on 10 uncertain questions will likely cost you 1 to 2 marks net. Those marks matter. A blank in the last minute is a smarter choice than a panicked guess.

Building your skip discipline during practice

You cannot develop skip discipline during the actual exam. It has to be trained during practice tests. Every time you take a mock CEE paper, practise the three-level system. Mark questions you skipped and review them after the test. Track how many of your guesses were right versus wrong. If your guess accuracy is below 40%, you are guessing too liberally.

I kept a small notebook during my preparation where I recorded every question I guessed on during practice tests. After five papers, I had data on 60 guessed questions. Thirty-one were wrong. That data convinced me to tighten my elimination threshold. By the time I sat for the actual CEE, skipping felt natural rather than like a sacrifice.

Subject-specific negative marking tips

Not all subjects carry the same guessing risk. Biology questions often have precise terminology, and the wrong options are usually plausible but subtly incorrect. If you know the topic even vaguely, elimination is usually possible. Physics numericals are either solvable or not, and guessing on a formula you do not know is almost always harmful. Chemistry Inorganic questions are the most dangerous for guessing because many answers depend on specific factual memory. If you do not remember the colour of a compound or the order of elements, guessing is a coin flip at best.

I developed a simple rule: be more willing to guess in Biology and MAT where partial knowledge helps with elimination, and be strict about skipping in Physics and Inorganic Chemistry where partial knowledge is often misleading.

The final calculation that changed my score

Before my second CEE attempt, I calculated something simple but powerful. If I skipped 15 questions I was unsure about instead of guessing on them, and my guess accuracy was around 35% on those questions, I would avoid losing approximately 7.5 marks from wrong guesses. Even if I gained marks from the 5 or 6 correct guesses, the net improvement from skipping was significant. On my actual CEE, I left 12 questions blank intentionally. My score improved by 19 marks compared to my first attempt. Not all of that was from skipping, but the discipline of skipping freed up mental energy for questions I could actually answer well.

Negative marking in CEE is not something to fear. It is something to use. Every blank question is a zero, and zero is better than negative. Train your skip reflex during practice, follow the three-level system on exam day, and trust the math. The students who score highest in CEE are not the ones who attempt the most questions. They are the ones who attempt the right questions.
