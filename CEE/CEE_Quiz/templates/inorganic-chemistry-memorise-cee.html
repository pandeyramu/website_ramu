Chemistry Inorganic Section: What to Memorise for CEE

Inorganic Chemistry in CEE feels like trying to memorise an ocean. But the exam only asks about specific pools. Here is what to memorise, what to skip, and the mnemonics that helped me retain it all.

By Ramu Pandey · Published: June 8, 2026 · 8 min read

Inorganic Chemistry was my least favourite part of CEE preparation. The syllabus felt vast, the facts felt random, and there was no logical framework to hang the information on the way Organic Chemistry has reaction mechanisms or Physical Chemistry has formulas. Every time I sat down to study Inorganic, I felt like I was reading a list of disconnected facts that I would forget within days.

The breakthrough came when I stopped trying to learn everything and started figuring out what CEE actually tests. I went through five years of papers and marked every Inorganic Chemistry question. The pattern was unmistakable. CEE does not test obscure facts or deep theoretical knowledge in Inorganic Chemistry. It tests a specific set of topics with predictable question types. Once I identified those topics, my preparation became focused instead of frantic.

The reality: 10 marks from a vast syllabus

Chemistry carries 30 marks total in CEE. Of that, roughly 10 marks come from Inorganic Chemistry, 12 from Physical Chemistry, and 8 from Organic Chemistry. The Inorganic section is significant enough that you cannot afford to ignore it, but it is small enough that strategic memorisation can secure most of those marks.

The key is accepting that you do not need to master Inorganic Chemistry. You need to score 7 or 8 out of 10 marks on Inorganic questions, and that requires knowing specific topics well rather than everything superficially.

The topics worth memorising

After analysing past papers, I identified four Inorganic Chemistry topics that appeared consistently and together account for roughly 7 or 8 out of 10 marks. These are the topics where memorisation effort translates directly into marks.

TopicFrequencyWhat to Memorise
Periodic PropertiesVery HighTrends in atomic radius, ionisation energy, electronegativity, electron affinity across periods and groups. Exceptions to trends.
Coordination CompoundsHighIUPAC naming rules, Werner theory, crystal field theory basics, isomerism types, common complexes and their shapes.
MetallurgyHighExtraction methods for Al, Fe, Cu, and Zn. Thermodynamic principles of reduction. Common ores and their formulas.
S block and p block elementsModerateDiagonal relationships, anomalous behaviour of first elements, common compounds and their uses.

The priority rule: If you have limited time, study Periodic Properties and Coordination Compounds first. These two topics alone account for 4 to 5 marks and have the most predictable question patterns. Metallurgy is next because the questions are formulaic. S block and p block are lowest priority because the questions are more varied.

Periodic Properties: the highest-return topic

Periodic Properties is the most rewarding Inorganic topic to memorise for CEE because the questions follow a pattern. You will be asked about trends across a period or down a group, and the answers depend on understanding the underlying rules rather than memorising isolated facts. The questions almost always involve comparing two elements and predicting which has a higher value of some property.

The specific properties that appeared most frequently in my analysis were atomic radius, ionic radius, ionisation energy, electronegativity, and electron affinity. The trickiest questions involved exceptions to the general trend, such as the ionisation energy dip between groups 2 and 13, or the electron affinity anomaly of fluorine. I made a single-page reference sheet with all the major trends drawn as arrows on a periodic table skeleton. This sheet became my most-revised Inorganic resource.

My mnemonic for remembering the order of ionisation energy across the second period was a simple sentence: Beryllium Boron Carbon Nitrogen Oxygen Fluorine Neon, with the dip at Boron and Oxygen marked with a star. Repeating this sequence ten times was enough to lock it into long-term memory.

Coordination Compounds: the naming and structure topic

Coordination Compounds questions in CEE are almost always about IUPAC naming or molecular geometry. If you can name a complex correctly and predict its shape, you can answer most questions in this area. The naming rules are fixed and learnable. The shapes follow simple rules based on coordination number.

The naming rules I memorised were: ligands are named first in alphabetical order, the central metal is named after the ligands, anionic complexes end in ate, and the oxidation state is given in Roman numerals in parentheses. For shapes, I memorised that coordination number 4 gives tetrahedral or square planar, coordination number 6 gives octahedral, and the choice between tetrahedral and square planar depends on the metal and ligands.

I created a list of ten common complexes with their names, shapes, and oxidation states. Practising these ten examples was enough to handle any naming question CEE presented. The key was writing them out from memory repeatedly until the naming pattern became automatic.

Metallurgy: the formulaic topic

Metallurgy questions in CEE tend to be straightforward. You will be asked about the extraction method for a specific metal, the principle behind a particular process, or the name of an ore. The questions are formulaic because the processes are standardised. This makes Metallurgy a high-return topic if you invest the memorisation effort.

The four metals I focused on were aluminium (Baeyer process for purification, Hall-Heroult for extraction), iron (blast furnace processes and the role of limestone), copper (Bessemer converter and electrolytic refining), and zinc (roasting and reduction). For each metal, I memorised the ore name, the extraction method, and one key chemical equation. This was sufficient for most CEE questions on metallurgy.

Mnemonics that worked for me: For the reactivity series of metals, I used "Potassium Sodium Calcium Magnesium Aluminium Zinc Iron Nickel Lead Hydrogen Copper Silver Gold" and created the phrase "Please Stop Calling Me A Zebra Instead Rather Let's Have Cup of Silver Gold." For metallurgy ores, I grouped them by metal and practised recall without looking at notes.

What to skip in Inorganic Chemistry

Not everything in the Inorganic syllabus is worth your memorisation time. Some topics have appeared so rarely in past papers that studying them deeply is an inefficient use of preparation time. Here are the topics I deliberately reduced my focus on.

  • d block and f block elements: Questions appear occasionally but are unpredictable. A surface-level awareness of common oxidation states and colours is enough.
  • Qualitative analysis: The detailed flowcharts for salt analysis are tested rarely and require extensive memorisation for minimal return.
  • Environmental chemistry: Usually appears as one easy question that can be answered with general knowledge rather than specific study.

Skipping does not mean ignoring entirely. It means spending 10 percent of your Inorganic time on these topics instead of 30 percent. Allocate the saved time to Periodic Properties and Coordination Compounds where the marks are more predictable.

Revising Inorganic in the last month

The final month before CEE is when Inorganic Chemistry revision becomes critical. The facts you studied three months ago are fading, and Inorganic topics are the most vulnerable to forgetting because they are the least connected to logical frameworks. My revision system was simple but effective.

In the last month, I revised Inorganic Chemistry every third day for about 45 minutes. Each session focused on one topic. I would test myself by writing down everything I could remember about the topic from memory, then compare my notes with my reference sheet, and then do 15 to 20 MCQs on that specific topic. Topics where I scored below 70 percent on the MCQs got an extra revision session two days later.

By exam day, I had revised each major Inorganic topic at least four times in the final month. The facts were fresh, the patterns were clear, and I walked into the Chemistry section knowing that Inorganic would not be the section that cost me marks. Strategic memorisation beats comprehensive studying every time in a section this size.
