How I Improved My CEE Score: A Practical Guide

My CEE score improved by 30 marks from my first full length mock to the actual exam. This was not magic. It was a systematic process of error tracking, targeted revision, and adjusting practice frequency at each stage.

By Ramu Pandey · Published: July 26, 2026 · 9 min read

When I took my first full length CEE mock test, I scored 98 out of 200. The result was discouraging, but the more valuable information was hidden in the details of which questions I got wrong and why. Over the next four months, I used that detail to drive every decision about my preparation. By the time I sat for the actual CEE, my score had risen to 128. That 30-mark improvement did not come from studying more hours or covering more topics. It came from studying more intelligently by fixing the specific mistakes that were costing me the most marks.

This guide is a stage-by-stage account of what changed at each phase of my improvement, what worked, what did not, and how you can build your own improvement process.

Stage one: the diagnostic (first mock to second mock)

After scoring 98 on my first mock, I spent an entire afternoon analysing every question I got wrong. I created a spreadsheet with columns for the question topic, the reason I got it wrong, the time I spent on it, and the correct answer. The analysis revealed something surprising. Out of 47 questions I got wrong, 22 were topics I actually knew but made careless errors on. These were calculation mistakes, misread questions, or answers I changed from correct to incorrect. The remaining 25 were genuine knowledge gaps.

The biggest revelation was that careless errors alone were costing me 22 marks. If I had simply read questions more carefully and double-checked my calculations, my score would have been 120 instead of 98. This realisation shifted my entire strategy. Instead of studying more new material, I focused first on eliminating careless errors.

The error categories: I classified my mistakes into four categories: careless errors (knew the answer but made a mistake), knowledge gaps (did not know the topic), time pressure errors (rushed and made mistakes), and guessing errors (blind guesses that were wrong). Each category requires a different fix.

Stage two: fixing careless errors (mock two to mock four)

For the next two weeks, I changed my practice approach. Instead of rushing through questions to maximise volume, I slowed down and focused on accuracy. I set a rule for myself: before marking any answer, I would re-read the question once and check my reasoning. This added about five seconds per question but dramatically reduced careless errors.

I also started a mistake log. Every question I got wrong in practice tests went into a notebook with the question topic, the error type, and the correct reasoning. By the end of two weeks, I had 60 entries in this log. More importantly, I noticed patterns. I was consistently making the same type of error in Physics numericals (forgetting to convert units) and in Biology (confusing similar terminology). Identifying these patterns allowed me to target my correction efforts.

The results showed up immediately. My second mock score was 108, and my third was 115. The 17-mark improvement in two tests came almost entirely from reducing careless errors. I had not studied any new topics. I had simply become more careful with the topics I already knew.

Stage three: filling knowledge gaps (mock four to mock eight)

Once careless errors were under control, the next bottleneck was genuine knowledge gaps. My mistake log showed that the most common knowledge gaps were in three areas: Physics mechanics (specifically rotational motion and gravitation), Chemistry Organic reactions (named reactions and their products), and Biology genetics problems (pedigree analysis and dihybrid crosses).

I allocated two weeks to each gap area. For Physics, I went back to basics and solved every NCERT exercise problem in the relevant chapters. For Chemistry, I created flashcards for each named reaction with the reactants, reagents, and products. For Biology, I practised pedigree analysis problems from five different sources until the pattern recognition became automatic.

Stage four: time management refinement (mock eight to final)

By mock eight, my content knowledge was strong but I was still losing marks to time pressure. I would rush through the last 30 questions in the final 20 minutes and make errors that I would not make with more time. This was not a knowledge problem. It was a time allocation problem.

I started applying the two-pass system strictly in every practice test. First pass through all questions, spending no more than 40 seconds on any single question. Second pass to return to marked questions. This system meant I attempted all the easy and moderate questions with adequate time and only spent extra time on difficult questions after the rest of the paper was done.

The effect was immediate. My mock nine score was 128, and the improvement came entirely from the second pass where I had more time to think about questions I had initially skipped. The two-pass system did not help me learn new material. It helped me use the material I already knew more effectively under exam conditions.

Practice test frequency matters: In the first two months, I took one mock test every two weeks. In the third month, I increased to one every week. In the final month, I took one every three days. The increasing frequency ensured that I was constantly testing myself and identifying new weaknesses before they became habits.

What did not work

Not everything I tried led to improvement. Here are the approaches that wasted my time or actively hurt my preparation.

  • Studying for 12 hours a day: I tried this for one week during my preparation. By day four, I was exhausted, my retention dropped, and I scored worse on the practice test than the week before. Sustainable study of 5 to 6 focused hours outperformed cramming every time.
  • Reading solution explanations passively: Early on, I would read a solution and think I understood it. The next time I encountered a similar question, I would get it wrong again. The fix was to close the solution, redo the problem from scratch, and only then check the answer.
  • Skipping the error log: For the first month, I did not keep a record of my mistakes. I was making the same errors repeatedly because I could not remember which questions I had gotten wrong or why. The error log was the single most impactful tool in my preparation.
  • Ignoring weaker subjects: I naturally preferred Biology and spent most of my study time there. But Physics was carrying the same weight in terms of lost marks. Allocating time based on weakness rather than preference improved my overall score faster than strengthening already strong areas.

The realistic timeline for improvement

If you are starting from a mock score of around 100 out of 200, here is a realistic timeline for improvement based on my experience and what I observed in other serious aspirants. The first two weeks should focus on error analysis and careless error elimination. Expect a 10 to 15 mark improvement. The next four weeks should focus on targeted knowledge gap filling based on your error patterns. Expect a further 10 to 15 mark improvement. The final two weeks should focus on time management refinement through increased practice test frequency. Expect a 5 to 10 mark improvement from better time allocation.

Total realistic improvement over eight weeks of focused preparation: 25 to 40 marks. This is not guaranteed, and individual results vary based on starting level, the amount of time available, and the consistency of effort. But the process works if you follow it systematically and track your progress with data instead of feelings.

The final lesson

The most important thing I learned from improving my CEE score is that improvement is a data-driven process, not a motivational one. Feeling confident about your preparation does not predict your score. Your error log predicts your score. Track your mistakes, categorise them, fix the most costly category first, and repeat. Every mark you gain comes from a specific, identifiable change in your preparation or your exam strategy. Make the change, track the result, and iterate. That is how scores improve.
