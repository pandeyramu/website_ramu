Organic Chemistry Tips for CEE Nepal

Organic Chemistry carries 17 marks in CEE, tied with Physical Chemistry as the largest Chemistry block. I avoided it for too long. Here is what actually worked when I finally committed to preparing it properly.

By Ramu Pandey · Published: March 21, 2026 · 9 min read

For the first two months of preparation, I avoided Organic Chemistry. I told myself I would come back to it once the other subjects were stronger. This was a mistake. Organic is worth 17 marks the same as Physical Chemistry and students who skip it or underprepare it are handing those marks to others.

The reason most students avoid it is that it feels overwhelming. Too many reactions, too many reagents, too many conditions. But most of that overwhelm disappears once you stop memorizing reactions in isolation and start understanding them as functional group transformations. That single shift is what made Organic manageable for me.

Think in functional groups, not chapters

The textbook organizes Organic Chemistry by compound class: one chapter for alcohols, one for aldehydes, one for acids. This makes it easy to learn each class in isolation but hard to see how they connect. CEE questions often test those connections: what happens when you oxidize a primary alcohol, or what product forms when an alkene undergoes hydration.

I reorganized my notes around functional group transformations instead. A single page showing: alcohol → (mild oxidation) → aldehyde → (strong oxidation) → carboxylic acid → (alcohol + acid) → ester. Once this chain is clear, related questions answer themselves through logic rather than memory.

The distinction test trap: CEE regularly asks which reagent distinguishes between two compounds: aldehyde vs ketone, primary vs secondary alcohol, etc. These are reliable marks if you have memorized the tests. Tollens and Fehling react with aldehydes but not ketones. Lucas test distinguishes primary/secondary/tertiary alcohols by reaction speed. Know the result, not just the name of the test.

A weekly Organic Chemistry routine that works

Day 6Distinction tests, named reactions, and IUPAC naming practice
Day 7Timed mini-test (20 questions, 15 minutes), update error log

The timed mini-test on Day 7 matters more than the days of practice leading up to it. Doing 30 questions without time pressure is different from doing 20 questions in 15 minutes. Speed and accuracy under time pressure is what CEE actually tests.

Common organic chemistry mistakes in CEE

After reviewing my own error log and talking to other CEE candidates, I noticed the same mistakes appearing across dozens of students. These are not knowledge gaps. They are pattern-level traps that CEE setters deliberately use.

The first major mistake is confusing oxidation products of alcohols. Many students know that alcohols oxidize but mix up which product forms. A primary alcohol oxidizes to an aldehyde and then to a carboxylic acid. A secondary alcohol oxidizes directly to a ketone and stops there. A tertiary alcohol does not oxidize at all under normal conditions. CEE loves to test this exact sequence with trap options that list ketone as the product of primary alcohol oxidation.

The second common mistake is misidentifying the correct distinction test. Students often know the names of tests: Tollens, Fehling, Lucas, but mix up which compound each test is for. Tollens and Fehling both identify aldehydes, not ketones. The Lucas test is for alcohols, not carbonyls. A simple swap in the question and the wrong answer becomes tempting.

The third mistake is forgetting reaction conditions. You might remember that an alkene undergoes addition but forget whether it is HBr alone or HBr in the presence of peroxide. That single detail changes the product from Markovnikov to anti-Markovnikov. CEE puts these condition-based traps in at least two or three questions every year.

  • Primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid (two-step oxidation)
  • Secondary alcohol → ketone (one-step oxidation, no further)
  • Tertiary alcohol → no oxidation
  • HBr alone → Markovnikov addition; HBr + peroxide → anti-Markovnikov

How to use reaction maps effectively in practice

Having a reaction map on paper is useful, but only if you use it the right way. Simply looking at it every day does almost nothing. The value comes from active engagement and testing yourself against the map.

I used my reaction map in three specific ways. First, I practiced forward recall: I covered the product side and tried to predict what forms when ethanol reacts with concentrated sulfuric acid. Second, I practiced reverse recall: I covered the reagent side and tried to remember which reagent converts propanol to propanoic acid. Third, I practiced chain recall: I picked a starting compound like methane and tried to trace a path to a distant target like ethyl ethanoate using only reactions I knew.

This chain practice was especially useful for CEE because some questions require you to think two or three steps ahead. If ethanol needs to become ethanoic acid and then ethyl ethanoate, you need to see both oxidation steps and the esterification step as one connected path, not three separate facts.

I also kept my reaction map on a single A4 sheet and pinned it above my study desk. Every evening before sleep, I spent ten minutes covering it and reproducing it on a blank page. The first week I could barely recall half the arrows. By the third week I could draw the entire map from memory in under eight minutes. That fluency transferred directly to faster MCQ solving because I was no longer searching for individual facts. I was navigating a map I already knew.

What to revise in the final week before CEE

In the final week, do not open the Organic Chemistry chapter again. Instead, review only three things: your reaction map, your exception sheet, and your personal error log of Organic mistakes. These three documents contain exactly the information you need and nothing extra.

If there are distinction tests you are still unsure about Tollens vs Fehling, Lucas test conditions, write them on one card and keep it in your revision kit for the last few days. These are easy marks that require only exact memory, and they are worth the targeted effort.
