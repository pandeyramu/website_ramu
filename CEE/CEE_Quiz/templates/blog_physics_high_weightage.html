High Weightage Physics Topics for CEE Nepal

Physics carries 50 marks in CEE and Modern Physics alone is worth 12, more than any other single unit in the Physics section. Here is how I prioritized each chapter and what I would do differently if I started again.

By Ramu Pandey · Published: March 21, 2026 · 10 min read

Physics was the subject I was most uncertain about going into CEE preparation. It felt formula-heavy and I had always been slower at numericals than at Biology recall. What changed my approach was realizing that most CEE Physics questions test a small set of formulas applied in predictable ways and that knowing which chapters to prioritize made a much bigger difference than trying to master everything equally.

The chapter that surprised me most was Modern Physics. It carries 12 marks, the single highest unit in Physics. Yet many students underprepare it because it feels abstract. Once I focused on it properly, it became one of my more reliable scoring areas.

Modern Physics, Mechanics, and Current Electricity together account for 31 out of 50 Physics marks. These three chapters alone deserve the majority of your Physics preparation time.

Modern Physics → 12 marks, highest in Physics

I avoided Modern Physics for too long because topics like photoelectric effect and Bohr's atomic model felt unfamiliar. The truth is that CEE tests Modern Physics in a very formulaic way, most questions are direct applications of a small set of equations. Once I memorized those equations and practiced the standard question types, this became one of the more predictable chapters.

  • Photoelectric effect → work function, threshold frequency, stopping potential
  • Bohr's model → energy levels, wavelength of emitted radiation, radius formula
  • Radioactivity → half-life calculations, decay law, types of radiation
  • Nuclear reactions → mass defect and binding energy
  • Semiconductor basics → p-type vs n-type, diode behavior

Formulas to memorize:

E = hf → photon energy

KE = hf − φ → photoelectric effect

N = N₀(½)^(t/T) → radioactive decay

E = mc² → mass-energy equivalence

Mechanics → 10 marks, the foundation chapter

Mechanics was actually one of my stronger areas because the concepts build on each other logically. Newton's laws lead naturally to work and energy, which leads to circular motion and gravitation. If you understand the logic rather than memorizing each formula in isolation, recall under time pressure becomes easier.

The question types I saw most in practice tests were projectile motion numericals, work-energy theorem applications, and circular motion problems. These three are worth drilling specifically.

Formulas to memorize:

F = ma

KE = ½mv²

PE = mgh

R = u² sin(2θ) / g → projectile range

v² = u² + 2as

Current Electricity and Magnetism → 9 marks

This chapter rewards formula practice more than any other in Physics. Circuit questions, Kirchhoff's laws, and electromagnetic induction are predictable question types, if you have practiced them with timed numericals, they feel routine in the exam.

The area where I kept making errors was sign conventions in Kirchhoff's voltage law. I added a specific note about this in my error log and reviewed it before every Physics test. A small fix but it stopped a recurring mistake.

Formulas to memorize:

V = IR

P = I²R = V²/R

F = BIL sinθ → force on current-carrying conductor

EMF = −dΦ/dt → Faraday's law

R = ρL/A → resistance in terms of resistivity, length and area

Waves and Optics → 8 marks

Waves and Optics combines conceptual questions (what happens when light passes from dense to rare medium) with formula-based numericals (mirror and lens calculations). I found the conceptual questions easier to secure quickly and the numericals more time-consuming, so I practiced the numericals specifically to reduce solve time.

Formulas to memorize:

v = fλ

1/f = 1/v − 1/u → mirror/lens formula

n = sin i / sin r → Snell's law

Heat and Thermodynamics → 7 marks

Thermodynamics is more conceptual than computational in CEE. Questions on the laws of thermodynamics, PV graphs, and kinetic theory are common. I prepared this chapter with a focus on understanding the processes (isothermal, adiabatic, isobaric, isochoric) and what each graph looks like graph-based questions appear regularly.

Formulas to memorize:

Q = mcθT

PV = nRT

θQ = θU + W → first law of thermodynamics

Electrostatics and Capacitors → 4 marks

Lower weightage but the questions are usually straightforward formula applications. I gave this chapter one focused day during consolidation phase, enough to be comfortable with Coulomb's law, electric field and potential, and capacitor combinations. I did not spend a full week on a 4 mark chapter.

Formulas to memorize:

F = kq₁q₂/r² → Coulomb's law

C = Q/V → capacitance

U = ½CV² → energy stored in capacitor

The one habit that improved my Physics score most: Keeping a formula notebook with every formula, its SI units, and the context in which it applies. I reviewed this notebook for 10 minutes before every Physics practice session. Forgetting a formula in a Physics numerical is a costly mistake, the notebook made it much rarer.

The Physics topic that surprised me: Current Electricity

I expected Current Electricity to be straightforward because the formulas are simple, V equals IR, power equals I squared R, and so on. What surprised me in the actual CEE paper was a question combining Kirchhoff's laws with a Wheatstone bridge circuit where the bridge was not balanced. I had only practiced balanced bridge problems during preparation, so when I saw unequal resistances in the arms, I froze for almost a full minute before realizing I just needed to apply Kirchhoff's voltage law to two loops and solve the simultaneous equations.

The question itself was not especially difficult, but it exposed a gap in my practice. I had drilled the standard textbook cases without ever encountering a slightly modified version. After that, I made a rule for myself: for every circuit topic, practice at least three variations, balanced bridge, unbalanced bridge, and a circuit with a mixture of series and parallel resistors alongside a galvanometer. That single change in my preparation made Current Electricity questions feel routine instead of unpredictable. If you are practicing this chapter, do not stop at the easy cases. Push yourself to solve problems where the circuit looks unfamiliar at first glance, because CEE loves to twist these just enough to test whether you truly understand the underlying laws or have only memorized patterns.

How I structured Physics preparation week by week

I started with Modern Physics despite it feeling unfamiliar, because the marks-to-effort ratio is the best in the Physics section. Once that was stable, I moved to Mechanics, then Current Electricity. Waves and Optics and Thermodynamics came next, and Electrostatics last.

  • Two days concept revision per chapter, with formula memorization
  • Three days of chapter wise MCQ drills, 30 questions minimum per session
  • One mixed Physics test per week under time pressure
  • Error log updated after every session, reviewed before the next

The weekly mixed test was what moved my Physics score the most. Drilling one chapter at a time builds knowledge but not speed across topics. Mixed tests build the kind of flexible recall that CEE actually requires.
