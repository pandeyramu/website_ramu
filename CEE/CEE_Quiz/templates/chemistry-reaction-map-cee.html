Chemistry Reaction Maps for Faster Recall

Instead of memorizing reactions in isolation, I built one map that linked every common conversion. That single page cut my confusion in half and made product-prediction questions feel obvious.

By Ramu Pandey · Published: March 21, 2026 · 6 min read

Organic Chemistry used to be my least confident section. Not because I had not studied it, I had read the chapters multiple times, but because every reaction felt like a separate isolated fact. When a CEE question asked what product forms when a primary alcohol is treated with a mild oxidizing agent, I would hesitate even though I had read the answer recently. The connection between "primary alcohol" and "aldehyde via mild oxidation" was not automatic enough.

The moment I stopped treating reactions as a list and started drawing them as a connected map, that hesitation went away. The map made the connections visible, and visible connections are far easier to recall under time pressure than lists of facts.

What a reaction map looks like

A reaction map is a single page where functional groups are nodes and arrows show the conversions between them. Each arrow has two pieces of information: the reagent and the type of reaction. That is all it needs.

Alkane →(halogenation, UV)→ Haloalkane
Haloalkane →(NaOH/H₂O, hydrolysis)→ Alcohol
Alcohol →(PCC, mild oxidation)→ Aldehyde
Aldehyde →(KMnO₄, strong oxidation)→ Carboxylic Acid
Carboxylic Acid →(alcohol + H₂, esterification)→ Ester
Alkene →(H₂O + H₂, Markovnikov)→ Alcohol

When I first drew this, I immediately saw why I was mixing up PCC and KMnO₄, I had been thinking of them as "oxidizing agents" rather than as "mild" vs "strong" oxidation with different products. The map made that distinction visible.

The two pages I kept

Page 1 → The reaction map: Full conversion chain for all main functional groups. I redrew this from memory every two days. If I could not reproduce an arrow, I had identified a gap.

Page 2 → The exception sheet: Reactions that break the general pattern. Markovnikov exceptions with peroxides. The fact that secondary alcohols oxidize to ketones, not aldehydes. The conditions where dehydration gives an alkene versus an ether. CEE regularly tests these edge cases because they are exactly the things students get wrong.

I reviewed both pages before every Chemistry test. Together they took about 8 minutes and covered the most likely traps.

How to use the map for active recall: Cover the product side and try to recall it from reagent and starting material. Then flip and cover the reagent and try to recall it from the starting material and product. Both directions appear in CEE questions.

What to put in the map first

Functional Group FamilyKey Conversions to Include
AlcoholsOxidation (mild → aldehyde/ketone, strong → acid), dehydration → alkene
Aldehydes & KetonesTollens vs Fehling distinction, reduction → alcohol, oxidation of aldehyde only
Carboxylic AcidsEsterification, reduction → alcohol, salt formation
AlkenesAddition (H₂, HX, H₂O, X₂), Markovnikov rule, ozonolysis products
HaloalkanesHydrolysis, elimination, nucleophilic substitution
AminesClassification, basicity trend, reaction with HNO₂

For each family, what I found most useful to include was not just the reaction but the specific condition that changes the outcome. For alcohols, I noted that concentrated H₂SO₄ at 170 degrees gives an alkene while at 140 degrees it gives an ether, the temperature distinction was tested in two separate CEE practice papers. For aldehydes versus ketones, I wrote a note that Tollens reagent and Fehling solution react with aldehydes but not ketones, which is the single most tested distinction in Carbonyl compounds. For alkenes, I listed the five addition reactions in order of how often CEE tests them: hydrogenation, hydrohalogenation, hydration, halogenation, and ozonolysis. For carboxylic acids, I included the fact that esterification is reversible and requires an acid catalyst, a detail that appears in assertion-reason questions. Having these specific notes inside each family cell on the map meant I was not just recalling the conversion, I was recalling the exact condition that the question is likely to test.

This is faster than re-reading chapters

I used to spend 2–3 hours re-reading the Organic Chemistry chapters during revision. After building the map, I could do a complete, active revision of all major reactions in about 20 minutes and the recall was better because it was active, not passive.

If you have not built a reaction map yet, spend one evening doing it. Draw it badly the first time, it does not matter. The act of constructing the map is itself a revision session, and what you produce is a revision tool you will use every week until the exam.

How to build your own reaction map step by step

If you have never made a reaction map before, here is the exact process I followed. I started with a blank A4 sheet, landscape orientation, because the map grows sideways as you add conversions.

Step 1: Write all major functional groups across the page as nodes, leaving enough space between them. I arranged mine from simplest to most complex, alkane on the far left, carboxylic acid and amines on the far right. This gave the map a visual flow.

Step 2: Draw arrows for the most common conversions only. Do not try to include every possible reaction on the first pass. I limited myself to the 12 to 15 conversions that CEE had tested in the previous five years of past papers. On each arrow I wrote the reagent above and the reaction type below.

Step 3: Mark the arrows in two colours. I used green for reactions I was confident about and red for ones where I kept forgetting the reagent or the product. The red arrows became my focused revision targets.

Step 4: After the first draft, I added a second layer of arrows for the less common but CEE-tested conversions like dehydration of alcohols to ethers at 140 degrees Celsius, or the Wurtz reaction for alkanes. These went on the map in pencil so I could erase and update them.

Step 5: Redraw the map from memory every three to four days. If I missed an arrow, I checked the textbook and added it. By the third redraw, I could reproduce the entire map in about 10 minutes, and those 10 minutes gave me a complete revision of all major Organic Chemistry reactions.
